# Understanding Equations (5) and (6) in the Occupancy Field

## Equations

$$
\Delta \dot p_j
=
\sum_{P_i\in P}
-k\,r_i
\max\left(
0,
\frac{1}{\|v_{ij}\|_a-\gamma}-b
\right)
\tag{5}
$$

$$
r_i
=
\hat v_{ij}
\max\left(
0,
\left\langle
\hat{\dot p}_j,\hat v_{ij}
\right\rangle
\right)
\tag{6}
$$

where

$$
v_{ij}=P_i-p_j,
$$

$$
\hat v_{ij}=\frac{v_{ij}}{\|v_{ij}\|},
$$

$$
\hat{\dot p}_j
=
\frac{\dot p_j}{\|\dot p_j\|},
$$

and the corrected output velocity is

$$
\dot p_j^{out}
=
\dot p_j+\Delta\dot p_j.
$$

---

## Core Interpretation

The Occupancy Field does not simply force the human to stop. Instead, it modifies the predicted joint velocity according to the positions of nearby occupied voxels and the current motion direction.

Its behavior can be summarized as follows:

1. Distant obstacles have little or no influence.
2. An obstacle affects motion only when the joint is moving toward it.
3. The correction becomes stronger as the obstacle gets closer.
4. The component of velocity directed toward the obstacle is reduced.
5. Tangential motion along the obstacle boundary can be preserved.
6. The final velocity is redirected to achieve smooth collision avoidance.

---

## Role of Equation (6): Directional Gating

Equation (6) determines whether the joint is moving toward a particular occupied voxel.

Let

$$
\theta_i
$$

be the angle between the current velocity direction \(\hat{\dot p}_j\) and the direction from the joint to the occupied voxel \(\hat v_{ij}\). Then

$$
\left\langle
\hat{\dot p}_j,\hat v_{ij}
\right\rangle
=
\cos\theta_i.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\max(0,\cos\theta_i)
$$

acts as a directional gate.

- If the obstacle is in front of the motion direction, then \(\cos\theta_i>0\), so the voxel contributes a correction.
- If the obstacle is approximately perpendicular to the motion direction, then \(\cos\theta_i\approx 0\), so its influence is weak.
- If the obstacle is behind the moving joint, then \(\cos\theta_i<0\), and the contribution is clipped to zero.
- The more directly the joint moves toward the obstacle, the larger the correction becomes.

Thus, Equation (6) can be interpreted as

$$
\boxed{
\text{obstacle direction}
\times
\text{degree of motion toward the obstacle}
}
$$

---

## Role of Equation (5): Distance-Dependent Repulsive Correction

Equation (5) sums the corrections produced by all occupied voxels.

The contribution of a single voxel can be written as

$$
\Delta\dot p_{ji}
=
-k\,\hat v_{ij}
\max(0,\cos\theta_i)
\max\left(
0,
\frac{1}{d_{ij}-\gamma}-b
\right),
$$

where

$$
d_{ij}=\|v_{ij}\|_a.
$$

Since \(\hat v_{ij}\) points from the joint toward the obstacle, the negative sign gives

$$
-\hat v_{ij},
$$

which points away from the obstacle.

Therefore, the correction generated by one voxel consists of three factors:

$$
\boxed{
\text{direction away from the obstacle}
\times
\text{degree of motion toward the obstacle}
\times
\text{distance-based danger level}
}
$$

---

## Parameter Interpretation

### \(k\): Stiffness Coefficient

The parameter \(k\) controls the overall strength of the correction.

- A larger \(k\) produces stronger collision-avoidance behavior.
- A smaller \(k\) produces smoother and weaker corrections.

---

### \(\gamma\): Human-Body Width or Safety Radius

The distance term uses

$$
d_{ij}-\gamma
$$

instead of the raw distance \(d_{ij}\).

This models the human body as having nonzero physical size rather than treating the joint as a point.

- A larger \(\gamma\) makes the controller more conservative.
- The correction grows rapidly when an obstacle approaches the body boundary.
- As \(d_{ij}\to\gamma^+\), the field magnitude increases sharply.

---

### \(b\): Effective-Range Threshold

The distance weight is

$$
\max\left(
0,
\frac{1}{d_{ij}-\gamma}-b
\right).
$$

A voxel contributes only when

$$
\frac{1}{d_{ij}-\gamma}>b.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
d_{ij}<\gamma+\frac{1}{b}.
$$

Therefore, the approximate effective radius of the Occupancy Field is

$$
R_{\text{effective}}
=
\gamma+\frac{1}{b}.
$$

- A larger \(b\) gives a shorter effective range.
- A smaller \(b\) gives a longer effective range.

---

## Combined Effect of All Voxels

Each occupied voxel \(P_i\) produces an individual correction

$$
\Delta\dot p_{ji}.
$$

All valid contributions are summed:

$$
\Delta\dot p_j
=
\sum_i\Delta\dot p_{ji}.
$$

For example, if most occupied voxels are located to the upper-left of the joint, their repulsive contributions will generally point toward the lower-right. The total correction will therefore redirect the motion away from the obstacle region.

---

## Formation of the Final Velocity

The corrected output velocity is

$$
\dot p_j^{out}
=
\dot p_j+\Delta\dot p_j.
$$

Here:

- \(\dot p_j\) is the original predicted joint velocity.
- \(\Delta\dot p_j\) is the correction generated by the Occupancy Field.
- \(\dot p_j^{out}\) is the final collision-aware velocity.

This vector addition typically:

1. reduces the component of velocity directed toward the obstacle;
2. preserves safer tangential motion;
3. redirects the joint away from occupied space;
4. produces strong deceleration when the obstacle is extremely close.

---

## One-Sentence Summary

Equation (6) determines

$$
\boxed{
\text{whether and how strongly the joint is moving toward an obstacle}
}
$$

while Equation (5) computes

$$
\boxed{
\text{a distance- and direction-dependent velocity correction away from nearby obstacles}
}
$$

Together, they ensure that only sufficiently close obstacles in the forward motion direction influence the joint, enabling smooth collision avoidance through velocity redirection.

Field regulation is a **differentiable collision-avoidance module** that operates on the **root horizontal-plane velocity** predicted by the network. It is NOT applied to individual joint velocities — it only modifies the character's overall movement direction in the XY plane.

This function transforms the global occupancy grid into the character's egocentric frame and produces `d_vecs` — direction vectors from the character center to each occupied voxel.

```
Input:  occu_g (global occupancy grid)
Output: d_vecs [bs, 15625, 3] — direction vectors, only XY components non-zero
```

### REAL_FIELD mode (default: `REAL_FIELD: true`):

1. Compute feet positions in global coordinates via forward kinematics
2. Center the field around the **average of left/right foot positions** (at the higher foot's height)
3. Only consider voxels between `higher_foot + 0.1m` and `higher_foot + 0.9m`
4. `d_vecs[..., 2] = 0.0` — **Z component always zeroed** (horizontal plane only)


## Step 2: Core Math — `project()`, `dist_func()`, `get_delta_v()`

File: [`training/utils/occu.py:565-622`](../../Motion-Occupancy-Base/training/utils/occu.py#L565-L622)

### 2a. `project(vdir, vecs, ignore_neg=False)` (lines 565-587)

Computes the vector projection of velocity direction onto each voxel direction:

```python
def project(vdir, vecs, ignore_neg=False):
    # vdir: [bs, 3] — velocity direction
    # vecs: [bs, n, 3] — direction vectors to occupied voxels
    
    dot_products = torch.sum(vdir[:, None, :] * vecs, dim=-1)   # ⟨v, d_vec_i⟩
    norms_squared = torch.sum(vecs**2, dim=-1)                   # ||d_vec_i||²
    projections = dot_products[..., None] * vecs / (norms_squared[..., None] + 1e-7)
    
    if ignore_neg:
        projections[dot_products < 0] = 0.0  # ← Eq.(6): max(0, ⟨v̂, v̂_ij⟩)
    
    return projections  # [bs, n, 3]
```

This directly implements the directional gating from Equation (6): when `ignore_neg=True`, only voxels the character is moving **toward** produce non-zero projections.

### 2b. `dist_func(vdir_proj, d_vecs, offset)` (lines 589-597)

Applies the inverse-distance penalty — the **code version** of Equation (5)'s distance term:

```python
def dist_func(vdir_proj, d_vecs, offset):
    dists = torch.linalg.vector_norm(d_vecs, dim=-1, keepdim=True)  # d_ij
    delta_vs = torch.zeros_like(vdir_proj)
    mask = (dists > offset).squeeze(-1)
    delta_vs[mask] = vdir_proj[mask] * 2 * torch.clamp(
        (1 / (dists[mask] - offset + 1e-4)**0.85 - 1.6), min=0.0)
    return delta_vs
```

**Paper vs. Code — distance function comparison:**

| | Paper Eq.(5) | Code (`dist_func`) |
|---|---|---|
| Formula | `max(0, 1/(d - γ) - b)` | `2 * clamp(1/(d - offset)^0.85 - 1.6, 0)` |
| Safety margin | `γ` (body radius) | `offset = 0.15` |
| Exponent | 1 (linear reciprocal) | 0.85 (sub-linear) |
| Cutoff threshold | `b` | `1.6` (implicit: voxel contributes when `1/(d-offset)^0.85 > 1.6`) |
| Scale factor | 1 | 2 |

The code uses a slightly modified distance penalty: a power-law falloff `1/d^0.85` that decays more slowly than `1/d`, plus a scale factor of 2. The `offset = 0.15` (≈15cm) is the safety margin γ. The effective range can be computed as:

```
d_effective < 0.15 + (1/1.6)^(1/0.85) ≈ 0.15 + 0.52 ≈ 0.67m
```

### 2c. `get_delta_v(d_vecs, v, offset)` (lines 606-610)

Orchestrates the full delta-v computation:

```python
def get_delta_v(d_vecs, v, offset):
    vdir_proj = -project(v, d_vecs, ignore_neg=True)  # NEGATIVE → repulsion
    delta_vs = dist_func(vdir_proj, d_vecs, offset)
    delta_v = torch.sum(delta_vs, dim=1)               # sum over all voxels
    return delta_v  # [bs, 3]
```

The `-` sign is crucial: `project()` gives the component of velocity **toward** the obstacle; negating it produces a vector **away** from the obstacle (repulsion).

### 3b. Forward pass with field regulation (lines 122-201)

```python
if d_vecs is not None:
    # 1. Extract predicted root velocity from network output
    v = x[:, 264:264 + 3].clone()          # [bs, 3] — next trajectory position
    v = denormalize(v, vel_mean, vel_std)   # back to real units

    # 2. Compute repulsive correction
    delta_v = self.alpha * get_delta_v(d_vecs, v, DIST_OFFSET)  # [bs, 3]

    # 3. Optional: clip delta_v (CLIP_DV: true)
    # Prevents over-correction: |delta_v| ≤ CLIP_COEFF * |v_proj_onto_dv|
    max_dv = -project(v, delta_v[:, None]).squeeze(1)
    norms_dv = ||delta_v||,  norms_mdv = ||max_dv||
    if norms_dv > norms_mdv:
        delta_v = delta_v / norms_dv * norms_mdv * CLIP_COEFF  # 1.05

    # 4. Compute auxiliary losses
    floss = (|delta_v| / |v|)²               # squared correction ratio
    vloss = |v|²                              # squared velocity magnitude

    # 5. Apply correction to trajectory and velocity outputs
    nxt_traj = x[:, 264:267]                  # next trajectory
    nxt_vel  = x[:, 264+future_kf*3:...]      # future velocity
    out_traj_vel = denormalize([nxt_traj, nxt_vel])
    out_traj_vel += delta_v                   # ← THE CORRECTION
    x[:, relevant_slices] = normalize(out_traj_vel)
```

### 3c. Full Training Loss (from paper & `train.py`)

The paper defines a **three-component** loss function (Equation 10):

```
L = L_mix + α·L_pen + β·L_field     (Equation 10)
```

#### Component 1: Motion Reconstruction Loss (Equation 7)

```
L_mix = ||y_θ - ŷ_θ||₁ + ||y_p - ŷ_p||₂   (Equation 7)
```

L1 loss for rotation-related outputs, L2 loss for remaining outputs.

#### Component 2: Penetration Loss (Equation 8)

```
L_pen = Σ_{i=0}^j O(p̂^{t+1}_j) · ||ref(p̂^{t+1}_j, O)||₂   (Equation 8)
```

where:
- `O(·)` returns 1 if the body part is occupied, 0 otherwise
- `ref(p̂_j, O)` computes the vector from joint `p̂_j` to the nearest **unoccupied** voxel center in `O`

This loss directly penalizes the predicted joints that fall inside occupied voxels, pushing them toward the nearest free space.

**Note**: `L_pen` is NOT implemented in the MOB codebase. The codebase uses a simplified loss without explicit penetration penalization.

#### Component 3: Field Loss (Equation 9)

The field loss has two sub-components implemented in the code:

```
L_field = (|Δṗ| / |ṗ|)² + |ṗ|²     (Equation 9)

where:
  floss = (|delta_v| / |v|)²    — ratio of correction to velocity squared.
                                   Penalizes large corrections, encouraging
                                   the network to naturally avoid obstacles.
  vloss = |v|²                   — squared velocity magnitude. Penalizes
                                   unnaturally fast motion near obstacles.

  FLOSS_RATIO = 0.2   (= α)      — weight for floss
  VLOSS_RATIO = 10.0  (= β)      — weight for vloss
```

In the codebase, the total loss simplifies to:

```
L_total = L1_loss + L2_loss + FLOSS_RATIO * floss + VLOSS_RATIO * vloss
```

### 3d. Ablation Study Findings (from paper Table 1)

The paper reports quantitative results from removing field regulation components:

| Configuration | Success Rate | PEN ↓ | FS ↓ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full model (with FR + L_field) | **77.91%** | 15.21 | 8.13% |
| Remove L_field only | 75.34% (−2.57) | 25.83 (+10.62) | 11.65% (+3.52) |
| Remove field regulation entirely | 77.38% (−0.53) | 33.49 (+18.28) | 6.57% (−1.56) |
| FR with weight=0.1 | 77.63% | 28.96 | 7.49% |

Key takeaways:
- **Without L_field**: field regulation produces "unnatural spikes" in velocity — the loss is essential for smooth behavior.
- **Without field regulation entirely**: penetration (PEN) spikes dramatically (+18.28), though foot sliding (FS) slightly improves — a clear **penetration vs. foot-sliding trade-off**.
- **The correction weight is tunable**: reducing field strength (weight=0.1) gives intermediate results, allowing the user to balance safety vs. motion quality.

## Step 4: Paper's Design Intent vs. Code Implementation

### Important distinction:

The **paper** (Section 4.4) describes field regulation as operating on **joint velocities**:

> `ṗ^{t+1}_j ∈ {ṗ^{t+1}_r, ṗ^{t+1}_j}` — can be either root joint velocity or **any body joint velocity**.

This means the method was designed to be a **general joint-level** collision avoidance mechanism, applicable to every joint in the kinematic chain.

However, the **code implementation** only applies it to the **root** (single point at the body center):

- `v = x[:, 264:267]` — only the root trajectory position is extracted
- `d_vecs` is computed from a single body-centered origin (feet center)
- No per-joint field regulation is computed

### What the field actually regulates in the codebase:

- **Root trajectory** (`nxt_traj[3]`): positions 264-267 of the 298-dim output — the character's next position in canonical (egocentric) space
- **Root velocity** (`vel[3]`): positions 264+future_kf*3 to 264+future_kf*3+3 — the character's future velocity

### What the field does NOT modify:

- Joint rotations (positions 0-198)
- Joint ego velocities / `jevel` (positions 198-264)
- Limb trajectory (position 270+4 onward)
- Any vertical (Z) component — `d_vecs[..., 2] = 0.0` and `v[:, 2] = 0.0`

### Summary: The code implements field regulation as a **root-level, horizontal-plane** collision avoidance mechanism. The paper's formulation supports extension to per-joint regulation, but this is not implemented in the released code.

# Discussion: Feasibility of Field Regulation in TextOp

## Question 1: Does TextOp Output Root Velocity?

**Yes — in the form of `delta_trans_local`.** The model outputs `[B, 8, 57]` features (FeatureVersion 3):

| Indices | Size | Content | Nature |
|---------|------|---------|--------|
| 0:4 | 4 | sin/cos of root roll & pitch | orientation |
| 4 | 1 | `delta_yaw` (yaw[t+1] - yaw[t]) | yaw rate |
| 5:7 | 2 | contact mask | binary |
| **7:10** | **3** | **`delta_trans_local`** | **root displacement/frame** |
| 10 | 1 | root height | scalar |
| 11:34 | 23 | `dof` (joint positions) | positions |
| 34:57 | 23 | `delta_dof` (dof[t+1] - dof[t]) | position deltas |

`delta_trans_local` at indices 7:10 is the root displacement per frame (20ms at 50Hz), proportional to velocity: `v = delta / dt = delta * 50`.

### Critical insight: coordinate frames ARE aligned

**Both `delta_trans_local` and the ego occupancy `d_vecs` are in the same frame** — the yaw-aligned body frame (character at origin, X = forward direction, rotated by yaw only).

- `delta_trans_local` is computed by rotating world-space displacement by inverse yaw quaternion (`motion.py:427`)
- Ego occupancy is centered on the robot, axes aligned with body heading

**This means NO coordinate transformation is needed for root-level field regulation.** The correction can be applied directly, exactly as in MOB.

## Question 2: Feasibility of Root-Only Field Regulation in TextOp

### The Decisive Constraint: Training Data

**The BABEL dataset (used to train TextOp's VAE) has ZERO occupancy data.** BABEL is a motion capture dataset (AMASS-based) — it contains human poses but no scene geometry. There is no `occu` or `voxel` field anywhere in the dataset pipeline (`dtype/dataset.py` has zero references to occupancy).

Occupancy is only available at **inference time**, provided by the controller (SONIC) via real sensor data.

This fundamentally separates the feasibility into two cases:

---

### Case A: Inference-Time Field Regulation ✅ HIGH FEASIBILITY

Apply field regulation as a **post-processing step** after VAE decoding. No training changes needed.

**Exact insertion point** — in `generate_dar.py`, after `vae.decode()` and before `reconstruct_motion()`:

```python
# generate_dar.py:151 — existing code
future_motion_pred = vae.decode(latent_pred, history_motion, nfuture=future_len)

# === NEW: field regulation (lines to add) ===
if use_field and d_vecs is not None:

    v_original = v.clone()
    v[:, 2] = 0.0  # zero Z for horizontal correction (like MOB)

    # Optional: clip delta_v

        max_dv = -project(v, delta_v[:, None]).squeeze(1)
```

**`d_vecs` computation** — add a helper to build direction vectors from ego occupancy:

```python
def compute_d_vecs_from_ego_occ(ego_occ, grid_size=25):
    """Build direction vectors from ego occupancy grid.
    
    ego_occ: [B, grid_size^3] occupancy values (0 or 1)
    Returns: d_vecs [B, n_occupied, 3] — grid coords of occupied voxels
    """
    # Reconstruct grid coordinates
    unit = 2.0 / (grid_size - 1)
    coords = torch.stack(torch.meshgrid(
        torch.linspace(-1, 1, grid_size, device=ego_occ.device),
        torch.linspace(-1, 1, grid_size, device=ego_occ.device),
        torch.linspace(-1, 1, grid_size, device=ego_occ.device),
        indexing='ij'), dim=-1).reshape(-1, 3)  # [15625, 3]
    
    d_vecs_list = []
    for b in range(ego_occ.shape[0]):
        occupied_mask = ego_occ[b] == 1.0
        d_vecs_list.append(coords[occupied_mask])  # [n_occ, 3]
    
    # Pad to same length or use as list of tensors
    return d_vecs_list
```

**Effort**: ~100 lines of new code. 1-2 days.

**Pros**:
- Zero model changes, no retraining
- Immediately testable with existing checkpoints
- Safe: can be toggled on/off at runtime

**Cons**:
- VAE decoder never learned to anticipate the correction
- Other predicted features (joint positions) unaware of root change → slight inconsistency
- No `floss`/`vloss` gradient signal to regularize motion

### Case B: Training-Time Field Regulation ❌ BLOCKED BY DATA

The architecture IS compatible:

```
Training forward pass (train_mvae.py:78-82):
  vae.encode(GT features) → z
  vae.decode(z) → future_motion_pred [B, 8, 57]
  ↓ INSERT HERE: field regulation on future_motion_pred[:, 0, 7:10]
  vae_manager.calc_loss(corrected_pred, GT) → L1 + L2 + KL + geo_losses
  ↓ ADD: floss, vloss to total loss
  loss.backward() → gradient flows through field regulation → final_layer → decoder
```

The gradient path IS differentiable all the way back:
```
floss/vloss → corrected δ_trans_local → final_layer → Transformer decoder
→ decoder_latent_proj → z → Transformer encoder → skel_embedding → input features
```

But **the BABEL dataset has no occupancy**. Without occupancy, there are no `d_vecs`, no field correction to compute, no `floss`/`vloss`.

**Workarounds (each with trade-offs):**

| Option | Effort | Risk |
|--------|--------|------|
| Use MOB dataset to train field-aware VAE | High (data pipeline + cross-dataset transfer) | Domain gap between MOB scenes and real robot scenes |
| Generate synthetic occupancy for BABEL (e.g., random obstacles) | Medium | Synthetic occupancy may not match real distribution → field regulation learns wrong patterns |
| Reconstruct scene from BABEL motions + scene datasets (PROX, etc.) | Very High | Complex pipeline, may overfit to specific scenes |
| Pretrain VAE on BABEL, fine-tune with field regulation on real robot data | Medium-High | Requires collecting substantial real robot data with occupancy |

**Verdict**: Training-time field regulation is architecturally sound but **data-blocked**. The most practical path is inference-time first, then consider fine-tuning if real robot data with occupancy becomes available.
